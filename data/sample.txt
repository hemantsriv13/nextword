The kettle clicked off just as the rain started again. I sat by the window with a notebook and a pen that was almost out of ink.
Writing is easier when the first sentence is allowed to be bad. The second sentence can fix it, and the third can move on.
Every morning I write three short notes: what I plan to do, what I am worried about, and what I want to remember.
The notes are not for anyone else. They are a way to see the day before it starts.
On the train, I read the notes again and cross out the worries that no longer matter.
Most of them no longer matter by the time the train reaches the city.
At work, the first hour is the quiet hour. There are no meetings and no messages, only the plan for the day and a cup of tea.
In the evening, I walk home the long way, past the bakery and the small park with the broken bench.
The bench has been broken for a year. Nobody fixes it, and nobody seems to mind.
When I get home, I open the notebook and write one more line: what went better than I expected.
Some days the line is short. Some days it is the longest line in the book.
The rain stopped. The tea went cold. The notebook was full, so I started a new one.
